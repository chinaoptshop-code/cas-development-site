Open-plan office acoustics: how to keep it quiet without solid partitions

CAS Development · 27 October 2026

An open-plan office gives you space and easy conversation, but noise quickly turns from an advantage into a problem. Here is how to plan open-space acoustics so the office stays quiet while remaining bright and open, without a row of solid partitions.

Where does the noise in an open-plan office come from?

In an empty room with bare walls, a concrete ceiling and glass surfaces, sound keeps bouncing around. A conversation at the next desk, a phone call, footsteps on the floor — it all layers into a background hum. People start talking louder, and the noise grows even more.

That is why open-plan acoustics is not a single panel on the wall but a set of measures: absorbing sound, diffusing it, separating zones and placing noisy spots away from where people need to concentrate. All of it is built in at the planning stage.

Which panels and ceilings absorb sound?

Soft, porous surfaces that dampen reflections work best. The ceiling is the largest free surface in an office, so that is where to start: acoustic ceiling tiles, suspended baffles, panels between the light fittings. Wall panels complement them where sound reflects the most — opposite team clusters and next to meeting rooms.

Panels can be part of the design: fabric, felt or timber-slat finishes. Colour and pattern are chosen to match the brand identity, so the acoustics do not look like a technical add-on.

Do carpets and textiles help against noise?

A hard floor reflects sound and adds footstep noise. Carpet tiles or rugs in work and break-out areas noticeably reduce the echo. Heavy curtains or fabric blinds, upholstered armchairs and sofas, and floor poufs help too.

Textiles work together with other measures, not instead of them: a carpet alone will not fix a high glass ceiling, but combined with ceiling panels it makes a tangible difference.

Can green walls and furniture act as acoustic screens?

Live plants and green walls diffuse sound and make the space cosier. They complement panels rather than replace them: they zone the space without closing it off and add greenery that does the team's wellbeing a lot of good.

Furniture can also act as a screen:

  • tall shelving units between teams that double as storage
  • desks with low acoustic dividers between workstations
  • soft modular sofas with high backs for informal conversations
  • cabinets and sideboards along zone boundaries instead of a solid wall

Why does an open space need focus rooms and phone booths?

Even the best acoustics will not make an open-plan office fully quiet if every conversation happens at the desks. That is why the open area needs small enclosed spaces next to it: focus rooms for deep work and phone booths for calls and video meetings. They take the loudest conversations out of the main space and do not require a separate large office.

Focus rooms and booths should be easy to reach: if the booth is a long walk away, people will use it less and talk at their desks.

Where should noisy zones and glass meeting rooms go?

Layout is the cheapest way to make an office quieter. The kitchen, break-out area, printers and shared discussion spots belong away from teams that need concentration, and not on the routes to their desks. Departments that talk a lot are grouped together, while those working on documents and code sit separately.

Glass-walled meeting rooms keep the light and openness, but glass reflects sound, so they need solid doors, seals and absorbent surfaces inside: a panel on one wall, a rug, upholstered furniture. That way the conversation stays in the room while the view of the office remains.

Questions and answers

Can an open-plan office be quiet without solid partitions?

Yes: we combine acoustic ceilings and panels, carpets and textiles, green walls and furniture as screens, and move loud conversations into focus rooms and phone booths.

What affects office acoustics the most?

The ceiling and the layout. An acoustic ceiling dampens reflections across the whole space, and placing noisy zones correctly removes much of the noise before any panels go up.

Do glass meeting rooms keep conversations private?

They do, if you provide solid doors, seals and sound-absorbing surfaces inside the room. The glass keeps the office bright, and the conversation does not spill into the open space.
