Office lighting: track systems, diffused light and work zones

CAS Development · 27 October 2026

Office lighting is not about the number of fittings on the ceiling but about a scenario: where people work at a screen, where they talk and where they relax. We look at the three layers of light, track systems and choosing light for each zone.

Three layers of light: ambient, task and accent

Even light from identical panels across the whole ceiling is the simplest solution, but not the best. In a well-lit office, light works in layers, and each layer has its own job.

  • ambient light — a soft base that helps people find their way and gives comfortable shadows;
  • task light — above desks, the meeting table and areas where people read, write or draw;
  • accent light — lighting walls, shelving, reception, green walls and brand elements.

Three layers give flexibility: when a corridor only needs the general level, accents and task light can be switched off, and during a presentation everything except the screen and the speaker's face can be dimmed.

Track and magnetic systems: when they make sense

A track is a rail to which fittings are attached and moved without reworking the ceiling. A magnetic track works on the same principle, but fittings are installed without tools and the profile is usually lower and neater.

Track systems are convenient where the office will change: the team grows, desks are rearranged, new zones appear. Instead of new wiring, you simply move a fitting along the rail. For accents — shelving, textured walls, green walls — directional track spotlights are also more practical than fixed recessed ones.

There are limits too: on a low ceiling a rail with visible fittings can feel visually heavy, and a black track on white becomes a graphic accent that has to be designed in deliberately. That is why we choose the system type at the drawings stage, together with the furniture layout.

Screen glare: where to place light relative to the desk

The most common complaint in offices is glare on screens. The cause is usually the same: a bright source sits behind the employee or is mirrored in the monitor.

  • do not place fittings directly above the screen or behind the employee — the reflection goes straight into the eyes;
  • choose fittings with a diffuser or deep anti-glare shielding of the source;
  • orient desks side-on to the window, not facing it or with your back to it;
  • add blinds or curtains so daylight does not compete with artificial light;
  • matt desk and wall surfaces cause less glare than glossy ones.

Light above a desk works better offset from the screen axis or running along the row of desks — linear fittings do well here. It is worth testing the layout on a mock-up before buying fittings for the whole floor.

Colour temperature by zone

Colour temperature is measured in kelvins (K). As a general guide rather than a standard: warm light around 3000 K feels cosy, neutral 3500–4000 K suits work. Harsh cold light above 4000 K is usually tiring in an office.

  • open-plan and private offices — neutral, around 3500–4000 K;
  • reception and waiting area — warmer, 3000–3500 K, so guests feel welcome;
  • meeting rooms — 3000–4000 K with dimming;
  • kitchen-café and break-out area — warm, around 3000 K;
  • corridors and toilets — neutral or warm ambient light without excess brightness.

The key is to keep one temperature within one space. A mix of cold and warm sources in neighbouring fittings looks like a mistake, not a design choice.

Light in meeting rooms and the kitchen-café

A meeting room has to work in two modes: a meeting at the table and a presentation on screen. So it is worth planning separate lighting groups — above the table, around the perimeter and accent — with a dimmer. For video calls, light should be soft and come from the front, not only from above, otherwise faces on camera will be in shadow.

The kitchen-café is where people step away from screens. Pendants above the bar or table, a warm temperature and several scenes fit here: brighter for cleaning and subdued for lunch. It is the same logic of layers, only with the emphasis on atmosphere.

Questions and answers

Which colour temperature is best for an office?

The generally accepted guide for work zones is neutral light around 3500–4000 K. For reception, break-out areas and the kitchen, something warmer around 3000 K is usually chosen. This is a guide, not a standard; the final choice depends on the furniture, walls and the purpose of the zone.

Why is a track system better than ordinary fittings?

Fittings on a track can be moved without reworking the ceiling or wiring, so a track suits offices that will change. The downside is that the rail is visible, so it has to be designed in.

How do you get rid of glare on monitors?

Do not place fittings behind the employee or directly above the screen, choose fittings with a diffuser, turn desks side-on to the window and use matt surfaces.
