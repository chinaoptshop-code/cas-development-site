What an interior design project includes: the full set of documents

CAS Development · 25 October 2026

An interior design project is much more than pretty pictures. Here is what a design project includes, which documents it consists of, how the crew uses them and why a renovation without working drawings almost always ends up costing more.

1. Measured survey and existing-conditions plan

Everything starts with accurate measurements: walls, windows, doors, heights, risers, ventilation, the electrical panel. They form the existing-conditions plan — the reference point for every later decision. An error of a few centimetres at this stage turns into furniture that does not fit its niche or tiles with ugly cuts. For a property abroad, a local surveyor can take the measurements using our checklist.

2. Space planning

The designer proposes several layout options: where the kitchen, walk-in wardrobe and workspace go, how you move between rooms. The chosen option is refined around how your family actually lives: how many people, whether you work from home, where to store things, a bike or a pushchair.

The layout has the greatest effect on comfort. So it should be approved before moving on to style and colours.

3. Concept and 3D visualisations

Style, colours, textures and lighting — as 3D images of every room. At this stage you see the future interior and make changes before approval, while changes cost nothing on site. Ideally 3D shows not just the overall look but the real materials and furniture that will go into the specification.

4. Working drawings

  • demolition and partition construction plan
  • electrical plan: sockets, switches, light fittings, circuits
  • plumbing and water supply plan
  • ceiling and floor plans with levels
  • wall elevations with tiles and panels
  • drawings for bespoke furniture

Drawings let the crew work without guesswork and without constant calls asking where a socket goes. Materials are also calculated from them, and completed work is checked against them.

The drawings must be consistent with each other: sockets on the electrical plan with furniture on the layout, water outlets with vanity and appliance sizes, ceiling levels with curtain tracks and ventilation. Then tradespeople do not solve these questions on the fly, and you do not pay for rework after finishing.

5. Specification and estimate

The specification lists everything that needs to be bought: tiles, flooring, paint, sanitary ware, light fittings, furniture — with product codes, quantities and location in the apartment. It protects against impulse purchases and quantity mistakes.

The estimate is the total cost of the renovation based on the design. If CAS Development carries out the renovation, the final cost stays within ±5% of the estimate. Some furniture, lighting and materials from the specification can be sourced directly from China — cheaper than retail.

Design supervision

During the renovation the designer visits the site, checks compliance with the design and helps solve questions that arise on site. It is a separate service, but it is what helps you get the result you saw in 3D. It is especially needed when the design includes complex ceilings, concealed doors, lit niches or a lot of bespoke furniture. For properties abroad, supervision runs online: we review photos and videos of key stages and answer the contractor’s questions.

Even with perfect drawings, questions come up on site: a wall turns out uneven, a material is out of stock, an appliance model changes. A designer running supervision quickly proposes an equivalent replacement so the interior keeps its intent.

Checklist: what to check in a design before renovating

  • is there a survey plan with heights and riser positions
  • is the layout with furniture arrangement approved
  • is there 3D of every room, not just the living room and kitchen
  • are there separate electrical, plumbing, ceiling and floor plans
  • are there wall elevations for the bathroom and kitchen
  • does the specification include product codes and quantities
  • is there an estimate, and what does it cover

Common mistakes

  • ordering only visualisations and expecting the crew to figure out the rest
  • approving 3D before the layout is final
  • buying materials before the specification is ready
  • making changes during the renovation without updating the drawings and estimate
  • skipping design supervision on a complex project

All these mistakes share one cause: decisions are made on the building site rather than in the design, when changing anything is already expensive. A full set of documents moves these decisions onto paper — where a change costs an hour of the designer's time rather than ripping out tiles or moving a socket. So when you choose an interior design studio and compare offers, look not at the number of pictures but at the list of drawings and whether an estimate is included.

Questions and answers

How long does an interior design project take?

It depends on the area and the number of revisions; we give exact timelines after the brief and fix them in the contract.

Can my own crew carry out the renovation from the design?

Yes, the project contains everything they need. If you prefer, our team can do the renovation with the estimate guaranteed within ±5%.

How does a design project differ from a 3D visualisation?

3D shows what the interior will look like. A design project adds working drawings, specifications and an estimate — the crew renovates from them and you buy materials from them.

Is the renovation estimate part of the design project?

Yes, at CAS the estimate is included in the full design project. If we carry out the renovation, the final cost stays within ±5% of it.
