Warm minimalism in interiors: how to make a space feel cosy

CAS Development · 21 October 2026

Minimalism is often seen as cold and empty. But warm minimalism in interiors can be very cosy — if you work properly with materials, light and storage.

What warm minimalism is

Classic minimalism means white walls, gloss, metal and almost empty rooms. Warm minimalism takes the essentials from it — clean lines, few objects, plenty of free space — but adds natural materials, soft textures and warm light. The result is an interior that feels calm but not sterile.

The palette is based on warm neutral shades: milky white, sand, warm grey, tones of wood and stone. Cold white and pure black are used sparingly, as graphic lines.

This approach works well in small apartments and in houses alike: fewer objects mean less visual noise, and natural materials add depth where classic minimalism would feel empty.

1. Natural wood

Timber wall panels, vertical slats and herringbone parquet add warmth instantly. In Linden Penthouse the slats also zone the space without solid walls.

It is better to choose one or two types of wood for the whole apartment and repeat them in floors, fronts and doors — that way the space looks coherent rather than assembled from different catalogues.

Wood pairs well with matt surfaces and microcement: together they create a calm backdrop against which furniture and light stand out.

2. Stone with character

Marble or stone with a striking pattern on a kitchen island, in the bathroom or on a TV wall becomes the main accent — almost no decor is needed around it.

Stone does not have to cover a large area. Sometimes a single slab on the island or a fireplace surround is enough to set the character of the whole space. The other surfaces are best kept calm so the accent is not lost.

3. Soft textiles

Generous sofas, wool rugs, linen curtains — tactile textures bring a space to life. Textiles are the easiest way to change an interior's mood with the seasons: swapping a throw or cushions is easier than repainting the walls.

To keep textiles from becoming a patchwork, they stay in the same warm palette as the walls, playing with texture rather than colour.

4. Concealed lighting

Linear lighting in the ceiling, niches and under furniture gives soft, even light. Evening lighting scenes matter more than a designer chandelier.

  • ambient — even, without dazzling points
  • task — above the kitchen worktop, by the mirror, at the desk
  • evening — lit niches, cornices and furniture in a warm colour temperature
  • accent — on a painting, stone or a textured wall

We plan lighting scenes in the design: niches, grooves for profiles in ceilings and furniture, and the positions of drivers and switches depend on them. Adding such lighting after the renovation is difficult, so it is planned together with the drawings.

5. One accent and closed storage

A large painting, a sculptural lamp or colour in the textiles — one strong accent instead of a dozen small things. This is how it is done, for example, in the Pidgirland and Diamond projects.

Minimalism relies on things not being left on display. So such projects include a lot of closed storage: floor-to-ceiling wardrobes with flat fronts, niches, hidden doors, handleless kitchens.

Open shelves are possible too, but in moderation: a few books, some ceramics or a single plant look like part of the composition rather than a storeroom.

Common mistakes

  • leaving only white and ending up with a “hospital” interior
  • removing all decor and textiles — the space becomes empty and echoey
  • skimping on storage and then cluttering surfaces with things
  • fitting one cold chandelier instead of several lighting scenes
  • mixing many types of wood, stone and metal in a small area

The main idea: minimalism is not giving up cosiness, it is giving up the superfluous. Everything that stays in the room should be of good quality and in its proper place.

Warm minimalism checklist

  • warm neutral shades at the base of the palette
  • one or two natural materials repeated across rooms
  • closed storage planned at the design stage
  • several lighting scenes with a warm colour temperature
  • soft tactile fabrics: wool, linen, bouclé
  • one strong accent in each space

Questions and answers

Is minimalism expensive?

Not necessarily: fewer objects, but better materials. Some furniture and lighting can be sourced directly from China.

Does minimalism suit a family with children?

Yes, if you plan plenty of closed storage and practical materials.

How is warm minimalism different from Scandinavian style?

Scandinavian style is lighter and simpler in decor, while warm minimalism relies on expressive natural materials, concealed lighting and almost no open shelving.

Which colours are used in warm minimalism?

Milky white, sand, beige, warm grey, tones of wood and stone; black only as thin graphic accents.
