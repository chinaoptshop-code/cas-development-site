Paying for a renovation in instalments: payment schedules and how they protect both sides

CAS Development · 1 November 2026

Paying for a renovation in instalments is normal practice that protects both the client and the contractor. But the payment schedule must be clear: what exactly you are paying for, what triggers the next payment and what happens if something changes. We go through common schedules, examples and the contract clauses worth checking.

Why split renovation payments into instalments?

A renovation is a long process with a large sum, and paying everything upfront is risky for the client, while doing all the work without payment is risky for the contractor. Staged payment balances these risks: the contractor gets funds to start and buy rough materials, and the client pays the next part once they see the result of the previous stage.

The second reason is the household budget. Splitting lets you plan spending over time: you do not have to hold the whole sum from day one, and you can match payments to other events — selling a property, a loan, bonuses. A good payment schedule is one that suits you and is clearly written into the contract.

Finally, staged payment disciplines both sides. The contractor plans the work so as to close stages completely rather than spreading out across the whole apartment. The client makes decisions on time, knowing the next stage depends on them. As a result, the renovation runs more smoothly, and disputes about “what has been done and what has been paid for” practically disappear.

Which renovation payment schedules exist?

At CAS Development we tailor the payment schedule for a renovation or construction to the client. These are the most common options:

50/50

Half the sum at the start, the other half at the final stage. A simple schedule for small, short scopes, such as a cosmetic renovation where there is little work and it goes in one block.

50/30/20

The first payment is half, then two parts are tied to stages agreed in the contract. Convenient for a turnkey renovation: there are funds to start, and the last payments motivate finishing the works through to handover without loose ends.

50/40/10

A variant with a smaller final payment. It is chosen when most of the scope falls in the middle of the project and only minor works, cleaning and handover remain at the end.

Payments split by stage

For large projects — houses, commercial premises, renovations with a long schedule — the sum is divided into more parts, each matching a specific stage: demolition, services, rough works, finishing, installation. That way payments follow actual progress as closely as possible.

Start from a 10% advance

If it is important for you to start with a minimal sum, a start from a 10% advance with further splitting is possible. The terms of such a schedule are discussed individually and likewise fixed in the contract.

Renovation contract, warranty and payment

What exactly are you paying for?

Before choosing a schedule, find out what the sum consists of. Confusion here is the main cause of conflicts: the client thinks they have paid for “everything”, while the contractor meant only the works.

  • works — demolition, services, rough and finishing works according to the estimate
  • rough materials — compounds, cable, pipes, waterproofing, profiles
  • finishing materials — tiles, floor coverings, paint, doors, sanitaryware, light fittings
  • furniture and appliances — kitchen, wardrobes, upholstered furniture, household appliances
  • extra services — designer supervision, online camera, cleaning, waste removal

At CAS, the apartment renovation rate from $550/m² covers works and rough materials. Finishing materials, furniture and appliances are separate, in the estimate. This means payments for the renovation and for buying finishing materials usually run as separate flows, and both should be visible in the schedule in advance.

Who buys the finishing materials

There are two approaches. In the first, the contractor or a procurement specialist buys the materials to the specification and you pay a separate invoice; this simplifies logistics, warranties on items and replacing defects. In the second, you buy them yourself to the design specification; you fully control the choice but take on delivery, checking completeness and timing. In both cases it matters that purchase dates are in the schedule: tiles or sanitaryware that arrive later than needed stop the crew regardless of who ordered them.

How do you calculate the base for a payment schedule?

Take an 80 m² apartment for a turnkey renovation. The guide figure for works and rough materials is 80 m² × $550 = $44,000. It is from this sum in the estimate that the shares of the chosen schedule are calculated — 50/30/20, 50/40/10 or another split. Finishing materials, furniture and appliances are added separately according to the specification, and their payment is planned for the moment of purchase.

This is only a guide: the exact sum comes from the estimate after the inspection and design. If your property has complex details — stone, hidden doors, relocated wet areas — they appear as separate items, and the base for calculating the shares changes. You can estimate the cost for your area in the renovation calculator, and how to read the estimate itself and what to check in it is covered in the article “How to read a renovation estimate”.

How do you choose a schedule for your situation?

The budget is already in place

If you have the whole sum, a schedule with a larger first payment such as 50/50 or 50/40/10 makes the start easier: the contractor buys rough materials in bulk straight away, and you have fewer payments to track. The rest of the funds can be kept for buying finishing materials and furniture.

Funds come in parts

If the money comes in gradually — from selling another property, a loan or a business — a detailed split by stage or a start from a smaller advance is better. Then the payment schedule can be matched to your income, and the work schedule to the payment schedule. The key is to discuss these dates honestly in advance so the crew does not stand idle.

A large project with a long schedule

For a house or commercial premises with several stages, it makes sense to split payment into more parts. Each payment becomes smaller and control becomes more precise: you pay only for what can already be checked on site or in a report.

How do you link payments to stages and acceptance?

The ratio itself says little if it is unclear when the next payment falls due. The most reliable approach is to tie each part not to a date but to the completion of an agreed stage that can be checked.

  • for each payment the contract states the stage and the list of works after which it is made
  • a stage is closed with a sign-off certificate or an agreed photo report
  • concealed works — electrics, plumbing, waterproofing — are photographed before being covered up
  • if the scope changes, changes are made as an addendum to the estimate before payment, not after
  • the final payment comes after acceptance, once the snagging list is closed

Regular reports make payment transparent: you see exactly what has been done at the time of each payment. At CAS photo reports arrive every 1–2 weeks, and for those who want to see the site live there is online renovation monitoring with a 24/7 camera for an extra fee.

Which payment terms are warning signs?

  • they demand 100% prepayment for the whole renovation
  • there is no estimate, only a “price per metre” and a verbal agreement
  • payments are tied to dates, not to completed stages
  • it is unclear what the sum includes: works, materials, waste removal
  • scope changes are “added on” at the end without agreement
  • payment in cash only without any documents

None of these signs on its own means the contractor is dishonest, but each is a reason to ask a question and request that the answer be put in writing.

What should the contract say about payment?

  • the total sum according to the estimate and what exactly it includes
  • the payment schedule: shares, stages, documents to close a stage
  • the procedure for paying for finishing materials, furniture and appliances
  • how additional works and scope changes are documented
  • the conditions under which the estimate may change and the limits of that deviation
  • the payment method and documents: invoice, sign-off certificates
  • warranty obligations after the final payment

More about the contract, warranty and payment procedure on the contract, warranty and payment page. The same principles apply to house construction: the longer and more expensive the project, the more important a detailed payment split becomes.

Before signing, it is useful to go through this list with the contractor's manager and ask them to show where exactly each point is written in the contract. If the answer sounds like “we always do it this way” but it is not in the text, ask for it to be added.
