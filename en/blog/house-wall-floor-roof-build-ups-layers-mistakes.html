Wall, floor and roof build-ups of a private house: layers, typical thicknesses, mistakes

CAS Development · 1 November 2026

A wall, floor or roof build-up is the order of layers that decides whether the house stays warm and dry ten years from now. A mistake in one layer is not visible at once: it shows up as condensation, mould in corners, wet insulation and heating bills. We explain what each construction consists of, which thicknesses are typical in market practice and where the build-up most often «breaks».

Wall, floor and roof build-ups: why so many layers

Every enclosing construction — wall, floor, roof — does several jobs at once: it carries loads, keeps heat in, stops wind, protects from water outside and from water vapour inside. No single material does all of this well, so the construction is assembled from layers, each responsible for its own task. The order of layers decides where moisture will move and where it can condense.

The main rule that explains almost every junction: vapour permeability of the layers must increase from inside to outside. Inside there is an airtight layer that keeps warm moist vapour out of the construction; outside there are layers that protect from wind and rain but let residual moisture escape. Done the other way round, moisture gets into the insulation and stays there.

  • load-bearing layer — masonry, frame, slab or rafters
  • thermal insulation — mineral wool, polystyrene, PIR or other materials per the design
  • vapour barrier or airtight layer on the inside
  • wind and moisture protection on the outside that lets vapour through
  • a ventilated gap where the facade or roof needs to «breathe»
  • finishes outside and inside

The thicknesses below are guide figures from market practice for Kyiv region conditions. Exact values come from the thermal calculation in the house design: it takes into account the specific materials, the climate zone and energy-efficiency requirements.

Wall build-up

Masonry with a rendered facade

The most common option for a house of aerated concrete or ceramic blocks. From inside out: internal plaster, masonry, adhesive, insulation boards, a base reinforcing coat with fibreglass mesh, primer and decorative render. The internal plaster is more than a finish here: a continuous layer without gaps works as the airtight layer, so walls behind sockets and furniture are plastered too.

  • masonry — typically 300–400 mm, grade and thickness set by the design
  • insulation — typically 100–150 mm; on aerated concrete mineral wool is preferred for vapour permeability
  • anchoring to the manufacturer's pattern, reinforced at corners
  • reinforcing mesh with overlaps, extra diagonal patches at opening corners

Ventilated facade

From inside out: finish, masonry, insulation on anchors, a windproof membrane or water-repellent boards, a ventilated gap and cladding on a subframe — fibre-cement panels, ceramics, timber boards. The gap lets moisture out and the cladding protects the insulation from rain and UV. The key is an open air inlet at the bottom and outlet at the top, screened against rodents and insects.

Timber-frame wall

In a timber-frame house heat is held by the insulation between the studs, so the quality of the layers is decisive. From inside out: finish (often plasterboard), a service void on battens for wiring, a vapour barrier with taped joints or a board with sealed joints as the airtight layer, the frame filled with mineral wool, a windproof board or membrane, a ventilated gap and the facade.

  • insulation between studs — typically 150–200 mm; an extra layer across the studs reduces thermal bridges through timber
  • the service void lets cables run without puncturing the vapour barrier
  • every pipe or cable passing through the vapour barrier gets a sealing grommet or tape

Plinth and apron

The bottom of the wall suffers most from splashes, snow and meltwater. The plinth is insulated with a material that does not fear moisture — usually extruded polystyrene — and clad with an impact-resistant finish. An apron sloping away from the house and drainage take water away from the foundation; if the apron is insulated, the ground near the slab freezes less.

Typical wall mistakes

  • dense polystyrene on aerated concrete without calculation — moisture is trapped in the masonry
  • insulating a damp wall after the wet season
  • gaps between insulation boards filled with foam or adhesive instead of inserts
  • a vapour barrier with untaped joints or punctured by fixings in a frame
  • a ventilated facade without wind protection or with a closed gap

Floor build-up

Ground-bearing floor and foundation slab

From the bottom up: compacted ground and a sand or crushed-stone bed, a concrete blinding or other base, waterproofing, extruded polystyrene insulation, a slab or screed, a separating film, a screed with underfloor heating pipes and the floor finish. In an insulated raft slab the XPS lies under the whole slab and around the perimeter, with heating circuits inside the slab. In a conventional slab the insulation goes on top, under the screed.

  • XPS under the slab or screed — typically 100–150 mm; the slab edge and plinth must be insulated too
  • waterproofing runs under the insulation and is turned up the walls
  • an edge strip around the screed, movement joints per the design
  • underfloor heating pipes are pressure-tested before the screed is poured

Joisted floor over a ventilated void

Typical of timber and timber-frame houses on piles or strip footings. From the bottom up: a windproof membrane or board under the joists, insulation between the joists, a vapour barrier on top, the subfloor and the finish. The void is ventilated through vents, otherwise ground moisture builds up under the house.

Intermediate floor

Here the task is sound, not heat. In a concrete floor a «floating» screed is laid on an acoustic underlay isolated from the walls; in a timber floor there is mineral wool between the joists, resilient ceiling hangers and an acoustic underlay under the flooring. Without this, footsteps upstairs will be clearly heard.

Typical floor mistakes

  • an uninsulated slab edge — a cold strip around the room perimeter
  • a screed without an edge strip: cracks and noise transferred to the walls
  • blocked vents in the underfloor void
  • a film instead of waterproofing under insulation on the ground

Roof build-up

The roof is where a house loses heat most actively, because warm air rises, and in winter the roof works longest under snow and temperature swings. So roof insulation is usually thicker than wall insulation, and the requirements for vapour control and ventilation are stricter. The construction is chosen depending on whether there will be a habitable attic, an unheated loft or a flat accessible roof.

Pitched roof with an insulated attic

From outside in: roof covering, battens, counter-battens forming a ventilated gap, a vapour-open breather membrane, insulation between the rafters, often an extra layer under the rafters, a vapour barrier with taped joints, a service batten and the finish. Under shingles a continuous deck of moisture-resistant board and an underlay are added, with the ventilated gap left under the deck.

  • roof insulation — typically 200–250 mm or more, the exact thickness comes from the calculation
  • the ventilated gap only works with an air inlet at the eaves and an outlet at the ridge or through vents
  • the membrane is laid the right side up with overlaps; the vapour barrier is taped to walls and rafters
  • insulation fits tightly between rafters, without gaps and without pressing against the membrane

Cold loft

If the loft is unheated, the floor under it is insulated rather than the roof slopes. The vapour barrier goes under the insulation on the room side, and the loft itself is well ventilated. This is cheaper and more reliable than insulating a roof that is not heated.

Flat roof

The classic scheme from the bottom up: load-bearing slab, vapour barrier, falls layer, insulation, waterproofing membrane. In an inverted roof the waterproofing lies under XPS insulation, with ballast or terrace paving on top. The most common flat-roof problems are at junctions: parapets, outlets, service penetrations and insufficient falls.

Typical roof mistakes

  • no counter-battens — the membrane lies on the insulation without a ventilated gap
  • a vapour barrier with breaks where cables and ventilation pass through
  • roof insulation does not meet wall insulation — a cold junction at the eaves
  • eaves vents blocked while finishing the soffits

Junctions where the build-up most often breaks

Assembling the plane of a wall or roof is not difficult. Problems appear where different constructions meet: the insulation and the airtight layer must pass from one to the other without breaks.

  • plinth and slab edge: floor, plinth and wall insulation form one continuous line
  • ring beam and lintels: concrete is insulated on the outside, otherwise it becomes a thermal bridge
  • windows: a three-layer installation joint and insulation on the reveals
  • wall-to-roof junction: wall insulation meets roof insulation, the vapour barrier runs onto the wall
  • service penetrations, balcony slabs, chimneys — sealing and thermal breaks

Airtightness and ventilation: the other side of the thermal envelope

The better insulated and more airtight a house is, the less air is exchanged through gaps. That is good for heat, but moisture from breathing, cooking and showers no longer escapes on its own. So the thermal envelope is designed together with ventilation: natural with well-planned inlets and extract ducts, or mechanical with heat recovery. Otherwise condensation appears on the coldest surfaces — reveals, corners, wall-ceiling junctions.

  • the design has a ventilation section or at least a supply and extract scheme for every room
  • extract from bathrooms, kitchen and laundry is mandatory
  • duct routes and penetrations through the vapour barrier are planned before finishing
  • in an energy-efficient house the airtightness of the envelope is tested before finishing works

How to read the build-up in the design

In working drawings the build-up is shown on sections and details: each layer is labelled with material and thickness, and junction details are drawn at a larger scale. If the design has only facades and plans, the crew will choose layers at its own discretion, and contractor offers cannot be compared. Before tendering, check a few things.

  • each construction — wall, floor, roof, intermediate floor — has its own section
  • insulation grades and thicknesses, membrane and vapour barrier types are specified
  • plinth, window, eaves and ring-beam details are drawn
  • the estimate has the same materials and thicknesses as the drawings

Checklist: how to check the build-up before it is covered

  • the design has sections with the build-up of each construction and junction details
  • materials on site match the specification by grade and thickness
  • insulation is laid tightly with staggered joints and no gaps
  • membranes are laid the right side up, overlaps taped
  • the vapour barrier is continuous, penetrations sealed
  • ventilated gaps have an air inlet and outlet
  • hidden layers are photographed with a tape measure before being covered

Questions and answers

What insulation thickness do private house walls need?

It is set by the thermal calculation in the design. In market practice it is typically 100–150 mm for a rendered facade on masonry and 150–200 mm between studs for a timber-frame wall, often with an extra layer. The exact figure depends on the wall material and the insulation.

Does an aerated concrete house need a vapour barrier?

A separate film in a masonry wall is usually not needed: the continuous internal plaster acts as the airtight layer. What matters is that vapour permeability increases outwards. In timber-frame walls and insulated roofs a vapour barrier with taped joints is mandatory.

Mineral wool or polystyrene for the facade?

For vapour-open walls such as aerated concrete, mineral wool is more often chosen: it lets moisture escape outwards and does not burn. Polystyrene is cheaper but on aerated concrete is used only by calculation. Extruded polystyrene is used for foundations and ground-bearing floors.

Why is a ventilated gap needed under the roof?

Through the gap between membrane and roof covering, moisture that has entered the insulation escapes, and the attic overheats less in summer. Without counter-battens and a free air inlet and outlet, the membrane gets wet and the insulation gradually loses its properties.

Should a foundation slab be insulated below or above?

Both work. Insulation under the slab keeps the massive slab inside the thermal envelope and suits underfloor heating in the slab; insulation on top is simpler on a conventional slab. In both cases the slab edge is insulated too, otherwise there will be a cold strip around the perimeter.

Can the build-up be fixed in an existing house?

Often yes: the facade can be further insulated, the roof re-laid, the floor redone. But a survey comes first — sometimes it is simpler to change the construction as part of a reconstruction. We decide after an inspection and calculation.
