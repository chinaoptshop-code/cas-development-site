Interior design contract: how to check a studio, its portfolio and red flags

CAS Development · 1 November 2026

An interior design contract is the document you will turn to months later to find out what exactly the studio had to do, how many revisions were paid for and who fixes a mistake in a drawing. We look at how to check a design studio before signing: what to look for in the portfolio, which clauses must be in the contract and its appendices, and which signals mean it is better to look for another team.

Why check a studio rather than choose it by taste?

A studio's style is visible at first glance at its website or social media. It is much harder to see whether it can produce documentation that lets a crew build the interior without improvising, and whether it is ready to stand behind its drawings. A mistake in a project is cheap while it is on paper and expensive when it is found after the tiles are laid or the kitchen installed.

We gathered general questions for the first conversation in the article 10 questions to ask a design studio. Here is the next step: how to check the answers in practice and what to fix in the contract so agreements do not stay verbal.

How do you check a design studio's portfolio?

A portfolio is more than a gallery of pretty pictures. It can be read as a report on what the studio can actually do. Here are five checks that take an hour and save months.

1. Visualisations or completed projects

A 3D visualisation shows the idea, a photo shows the result. Ask to see at least a few completed interiors, ideally next to the renders of the same room. If the photos are close to the 3D, the project was well thought through and the renovation followed the drawings. If there are no completed works at all, that is not necessarily bad, but then look especially closely at the working documentation.

2. A sample of working drawings

Ask to see a few sheets from a real project: the socket and switch layout, the ceiling plan, a bathroom elevation with the tile layout, a specification. They show whether levels, dimensions, product codes and details are given. We covered the full list of documents in the article what an interior design project includes.

3. Similar tasks

A new-build apartment, a house, a commercial space and a penthouse have different difficulties: services, re-planning, sound insulation, procurement. Look in the portfolio for projects similar to yours in area and task, and ask what was hardest in them.

4. Variety of solutions

If all the studio's projects look the same in palette and furniture, your apartment will probably be fitted into the same template. Different interiors for different people are a sign that the studio works from the brief, not from its own favourite style.

5. A live site or a conversation with a client

Sometimes studios can show a project in progress or give the contact of a client who agreed to share their experience. Not all clients agree to this, and a refusal for privacy reasons is normal. But if the studio cannot show any confirmation of real work, be cautious.

Who will work on your project?

A design project is teamwork, even if one person is the face of the studio. Ask who is responsible for each stage and who will be your contact. A full-cycle studio usually involves:

  • a designer or architect who leads the concept and layout decisions
  • a visualiser for the 3D
  • a specialist in working documentation and engineering sections
  • a procurement specialist who selects materials, furniture and lighting within the budget
  • an estimator, if the studio also carries out the renovation
  • a project manager — one person who answers your questions

Separately, clarify who takes the measurements: everything that follows depends on their accuracy. If a third-party contractor does the measuring, the contract must make clear who is responsible for errors in it.

Why do measurements and a brief come before the contract?

A good contract rests on two source documents: the measurements and the brief. The measurements record the real geometry of the space — wall curvature, heights, positions of risers, vent ducts, windows and radiators. The brief records your requirements: who will live there, which zones are needed, what to store, how you cook, work from home, host guests. If a studio offers to start drawing 3D straight away without these two steps, it is working blind.

It is worth agreeing that the brief becomes an appendix to the contract or is at least approved in writing. Then a dispute of the “that's not what we agreed” kind is settled by referring to the document, not to people's memory.

What should an interior design contract contain?

Below are the clauses worth finding in the contract or its appendices. The wording may differ, but the substance must be in writing.

Subject and scope

  • the address and area of the property on which the price is based
  • an appendix listing the documents: measured survey, layout options, 3D visualisations, working drawings, specifications
  • the number of layout options and the number of rooms with 3D
  • the delivery format: PDF, print, whether source files are handed over

Stages, approvals and revisions

  • the stages — layout, concept, working documentation, procurement — and the result of each
  • how a stage is approved: by signature, email or messenger
  • how many rounds of revisions are included at each stage
  • what counts as new work: changing an already approved layout after moving on to drawings

Timing and payment

  • deadlines by stage, and the fact that a deadline shifts if the client takes long to approve
  • staged payment: an advance and payments after each stage is approved
  • what happens on termination: payment for completed stages and handover of finished materials

Designer supervision and liability

  • whether designer supervision is included, how many site visits, how extra visits are charged
  • who fixes a mistake in the drawings and at whose expense
  • copyright: use of the project for your property, publication of photos in the portfolio only with your consent
  • confidentiality of the address and personal data

Designer supervision is not “the designer drops by sometimes”. It means the author of the project checks compliance with the drawings on site at key moments — after partitions are set out, before plastering and closing up services, before tiling, during furniture and lighting installation — and promptly answers the crew's questions. Without it even accurate documentation gradually “drifts” through small decisions made on site.

Design-only or full-cycle studio: which to choose?

A studio that does only the project hands you the documentation, and another team then runs the renovation. This works if you are ready to coordinate the crew, purchasing and supervision yourself. A full-cycle studio takes on the project, the estimate and the implementation. The advantage is not “everything in one pair of hands” as a slogan, but concrete things: the designer and site manager discuss details before the start, the estimate is calculated from the same drawings, and site questions are not bounced between contractors.

If you choose design only, complete working documentation, specifications with product codes and a clearly described designer supervision are especially important in the contract. If you choose the full cycle, ask for a sample estimate and how the renovation price is fixed.

What should you receive at the end?

When the project is complete, you should have a set you can take to any qualified crew and get the same result: an album of drawings with numbered sheets, 3D visualisations of the approved rooms, and specifications of materials, furniture, lighting and sanitaryware with product codes and quantities. It is good when the studio also hands over a list of changes made during supervision, so the documentation matches what was actually built.

When is it better not to sign?

No single signal means a studio is bad. But if several coincide, the risk grows.

  • a price per m² with no list of documents — it is unclear what you are paying for
  • 100% payment up front for the whole project with no stages
  • “drawings aren't needed, the crew will figure it out” — a direct path to improvising on site
  • refusal to show a sample of working documentation
  • unlimited revisions in words and no number in the contract — revisions later become a dispute
  • no clause on correcting mistakes in the drawings
  • the studio does not ask about the budget for renovation and furniture — the project may not fit the money
  • a promise of an exact renovation sum before the project and measurements

Studio prices can only be compared together with the scope of work. A cheaper offer often means fewer drawings, no specifications or no supervision, and then part of the decisions will be made by the crew. Think separately about the cost of mistakes. The difference between a cheaper and a more complete project is often smaller than the price of one redo: a socket moved behind the kitchen, tiles relaid because of a forgotten access panel, a kitchen that did not fit its niche. So the composition of the documentation deserves more attention than the rate per metre.

  • put the offers in a table: documents, number of 3D views, revisions, supervision, timing
  • compare the cost for the same scope, not per m²
  • ask what is charged separately: measurements, site visits, extra rooms
  • find out whether the studio can carry out the renovation and whether it guarantees the estimate

How do you avoid typical conflicts with a studio?

  • “We didn't order this option” — approve each stage in writing, not verbally
  • “The revisions have run out” — know the limit in advance and collect comments in one list
  • “The drawings don't match the site” — check who took the measurements and who is responsible for them
  • “The project is turning out more expensive than we thought” — state the renovation and furniture budget at the start
  • “The deadlines were missed” — the contract should have deadlines for both the studio and your approvals

If a problem does arise, record it in writing, referring to the contract clause and the drawing sheet. Most issues are resolved at this level if the documents give you something to refer to.

What should you check before signing the contract?

  • you have seen completed projects or confirmation of real work
  • you have seen a sample of working drawings and specifications
  • the contract has an appendix with the project scope
  • the number of revisions and the stage approval procedure are written down
  • payment is staged and the termination terms are clear
  • designer supervision is described: format, number of visits, cost
  • you know who your manager is and how you will receive reports
  • you discussed the budget for renovation and furniture, not just for the project

Questions and answers

What must an interior design contract contain?

The list of documents in an appendix, the stages and their approval procedure, the number of revisions, deadlines by stage, staged payment, the terms of designer supervision, liability for mistakes in the drawings and the termination procedure.

How can I check that a studio can produce working drawings?

Ask for a few sheets from a real project: the socket and switch plan, the ceiling plan, a bathroom elevation, a specification. They should show levels, dimensions, product codes and details.

Is it normal to pay 100% up front for a design project?

Staged payment is safer: an advance and payments after each stage is approved. That way you pay for the result received, and the studio is motivated to deliver stages on time.

How many revisions should be included in the price?

It varies between studios. What matters is that the number is written in the contract for each stage, and that it is clear that changing an already approved stage is paid separately.
