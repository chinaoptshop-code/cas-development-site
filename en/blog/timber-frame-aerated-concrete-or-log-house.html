Timber frame, aerated concrete or timber beams: what to build a house from

CAS Development · 1 November 2026

There is no single right answer to “timber frame, aerated concrete or timber beams”: each technology has its strengths and its requirements, so future house owners choose the material to suit their plot, timing and budget. We compare them on what really affects life in the house and the budget, and explain why the decision should be made together with the design, not instead of it.

What should you build a house from: frame, aerated concrete or timber beams?

The wall material is chosen not by asking which technology is always better, but as an answer to several specific questions: how you will live in the house, where the plot is, what the timing and budget are, how much warmth, quiet and minimal maintenance matter. Once the answers are clear, the choice usually narrows to one or two options.

  • will you live in the house year-round or seasonally
  • what soils are on the plot and which foundation they allow
  • how important it is to move in sooner
  • what facade and interior finishes you want
  • whether you plan to extend the house in future
  • how much you are prepared to invest in maintenance over the years

Below is a comparison of the three most common technologies for a private house. We deliberately give no thermal conductivity or timing figures for each material: they depend on the specific products, thicknesses, insulation and design, and without a design such figures mislead.

What is a timber-frame house and who is it for?

A timber-frame house is a load-bearing timber or steel frame whose spaces between studs are filled with insulation and closed with boards and finishes inside and out. Warmth in such a house comes from the insulation layer, so the quality of installation and the vapour barrier is decisive.

Strengths

  • a light structure — lower demands on the foundation
  • a mostly dry technology with fewer wet processes and technological pauses
  • a thick insulation layer is easy to include
  • services are conveniently run inside the walls
  • flexibility in layout and architecture, including A-frame houses

What to watch

  • the quality of the vapour barrier and junctions: mistakes there show up later
  • sound insulation of partitions and floors is designed separately
  • frame timber must be dry and treated
  • heavy wall-mounted items need fixings built into the frame

A frame is often chosen for a country house, a holiday home or a guest house on the plot, and when it matters to reach finishing sooner. But the timber-frame technology also works well for year-round living if the design thinks through insulation, ventilation and sound insulation, and the crew follows the technology at the junctions.

What is an aerated concrete house and who is it for?

Aerated concrete is lightweight cellular concrete in large blocks. Walls are laid with adhesive and then insulated and finished according to the design. For many clients aerated concrete is the familiar “masonry” house: solid walls that easily take furniture and appliances, and a technology most crews understand.

Strengths

  • solid walls that store heat
  • a laying technology most crews understand
  • a non-combustible mineral material
  • walls hold wall-mounted furniture, kitchens and appliances well
  • a wide choice of facades: render, cladding, ventilated facade

What to watch

  • it needs a reliable foundation and reinforcement per the design
  • aerated concrete does not like prolonged wetting — the facade and plinth must be protected
  • wet processes — plaster, screed — add technological pauses
  • facade insulation is usually needed, and its thickness is set by the design

If you are torn between aerated concrete and ceramic blocks, that is a separate question, also decided in the design taking into account loads, facade and budget.

What is a timber-beam house and why do people choose it?

A timber-beam house has walls of solid or glued laminated timber. People choose it for the atmosphere: natural wood inside and out, a characteristic scent, a warm texture. But wood is a living material, and a timber-beam house requires care at every stage.

Strengths

  • a natural material with strong aesthetics — often with no extra wall finish
  • a relatively light structure compared with masonry
  • a pleasant indoor climate valued by country house owners
  • glued laminated beams are more stable than solid ones and deform less

What to watch

  • shrinkage: especially in solid beams — it is allowed for in window, door and partition details
  • protection against moisture, insects and fire — treatment to the manufacturer's technology
  • regular facade maintenance over the years
  • services must be routed so as not to weaken the walls or hide mistakes under the wood

Timber beams suit those for whom natural wood is the main value of the house and who are ready for regular maintenance. If you only want to see wood in the interior, it can be used in the finishes of a house built with any other technology.

Besides the walls, what affects warmth in a house?

Besides the walls, windows, roof, insulation of the foundation and plinth, ventilation and the heating system affect warmth and bills just as much. A house with the best walls but cold junctions around windows and no well-planned ventilation will be uncomfortable.

  • ventilation: in an airtight insulated house without it, condensation and stuffiness appear
  • window and roof junctions are the most common places of heat loss
  • the heating system is sized to the heat losses of the specific design
  • backup power and water supply for a country house are worth planning from the start

So the engineering sections are best designed together with the architecture and structure — as part of house design. Then the choice of wall material becomes part of a coherent solution, not a separate bet on which everything else depends.

Comparison: a checklist for choosing

  • Speed on site: frame and timber beams — mostly dry assembly; aerated concrete — more wet processes
  • Foundation: frame and timber beams are lighter; aerated concrete needs a more massive base
  • Warmth: in a frame — the insulation layer; in aerated concrete — the wall mass plus insulation per the design; in timber beams — the wood thickness and insulated junctions
  • Finishes: frame and aerated concrete — any; timber beams are often left exposed
  • Maintenance: lowest for aerated concrete with a good facade; highest for exposed wood
  • Wall-mounted furniture and appliances: aerated concrete — no limits; frame and timber beams — built-in fixings per the design
  • Risks: frame — vapour barrier quality; aerated concrete — moisture and cracks on a weak base; timber beams — shrinkage and wood protection

A separate option is a modular house, where part of the structure is made off site. It suits cases where speed and predictability matter and the architecture can be more restrained.

Plot and soils: what to check before choosing

Sometimes the plot decides for you. Weak or waterlogged soils, a slope, difficult access for machinery — all affect the foundation and logistics, and hence the choice of technology.

  • site geology: soil type and groundwater level
  • access for lorries and a crane: important for blocks, beams and ready modules
  • the possibility of connecting electricity, gas, water and drainage
  • building restrictions, boundary setbacks and height limits
  • orientation — for windows, terraces and sun in the rooms

This data is best obtained before the sketch design. A geological survey and topographic survey cost far less than redoing the foundation or design after it turns out the soil will not carry heavy walls or water lies close to the surface.

What does a house cost and what does the price depend on?

The wall material affects the budget less than it seems. The area and shape of the house, a foundation for the specific soils, the roof, windows, services and level of finishes weigh much more. So comparing “the price of aerated concrete with the price of a frame” without a design is almost pointless.

What mistakes are made when choosing a technology?

  • choosing the material on friends' advice without considering soils and the design
  • saving on the foundation to “fit” the chosen material
  • planning a timber-beam house without allowing for shrinkage at windows and partitions
  • building a frame without a well-thought-out vapour barrier and ventilation
  • not including facade insulation of aerated concrete in the budget from the start

Questions and answers

Which is better for year-round living — timber frame or aerated concrete?

Both technologies suit year-round living with the right design and insulation. The choice depends on the soils, budget, timing and the finishes you want.

Is a timber-frame house faster to build?

Frame and timber beams are mostly dry technologies with fewer technological pauses. But the overall timing depends on the design, foundation, services and finishes.

Does a timber-beam house shrink?

Yes, especially one of solid beams. Shrinkage is allowed for in window, door and partition details; glued laminated beams are more stable.

Can the wall material be chosen without a design?

Preliminarily, yes, but the final decision is better made together with the design: it takes into account soils, loads, insulation and budget.
