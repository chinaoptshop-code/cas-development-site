Space planning: when it is enough and when you need a full design project

CAS Development · 1 November 2026

A space plan for an apartment or house costs from $6/m² and is the shortest route from bare walls to a clear plan: where the walls, the furniture and the wet areas go. Sometimes that is enough, sometimes not. Here is what the planning package includes, when it is sufficient and when saving on drawings turns into rework.

What is a space plan?

A space plan is the first and most important part of any design project. It answers the question "how do we fit our life into these square metres": where the walls and doors go, how many bedrooms there will be, where the kitchen, wardrobe and bathrooms sit, how the furniture stands and how people move through the space.

Unlike a full project, it has no 3D visualisations, wall elevations, electrical drawings or tile layouts. But it has the main thing: verified geometry. A layout mistake cannot be fixed later with wall colour or expensive furniture, so even a short planning package is better than renovating by eye.

What does the planning package include?

CAS Development has three standard design packages, and planning is the shortest of them. It includes:

  • a measured survey — exact dimensions of walls, openings, risers and windows
  • 2–3 layout options to choose from
  • a furniture layout for the chosen option
  • a room schedule with areas

That is enough to see how the space will work, agree the number of rooms and storage, and show the plan to a crew, a furniture factory or an estate agent. For a complex renovation it is too little — more on that below.

When is a space plan enough?

  • you are buying an apartment or house and want to check that it fits the rooms you need
  • you plan a cosmetic renovation without moving walls, wet areas or wiring
  • you are preparing a rental with simple finishes and standard furniture
  • you want to compare several zoning scenarios before deciding on the full project
  • you already have a designer or architect who only needs verified geometry
  • a house or large commercial space where functional zones must be agreed first

In all these cases the key decision is geometric. If the crew has nothing to guess after it, you can skip the full package.

Planning a house or a large area

For a house, planning as a separate step is especially useful. On a large area it is easy to lose the logic: bedrooms end up next to the living room, wardrobes far from bedrooms, plant rooms in the best corners with windows. First agree how the zones connect — entrance, kitchen, terrace, guest wing, private wing, boiler room, laundry and storerooms. Once the whole family is happy with that scheme, moving on to visualisations and drawings is much easier.

How does the planning work go?

  1. Brief: family, daily routines, wishes for rooms and storage, budget.
  2. Survey: walls, openings, risers, ventilation shafts, ceiling heights.
  3. 2–3 layout options with furniture, compared together with the designer.
  4. Refining the chosen option: passages, doors, wardrobe sizes, appliance spaces.
  5. Final plan, furniture layout and room schedule — a document for the crew or the basis of a full project.

Do not rush the comparison. Walk through the plan in your head: where you take off your shoes, where you put the shopping bags, how a child gets from the bathroom to the bedroom, where laundry dries. Everyday scenarios like these show which option works and which only looks good on paper.

When do you need a full design project?

As soon as the renovation touches engineering or involves bespoke furniture, a plan alone is not enough. The crew will ask questions the plan does not answer: socket heights, water outlets, ceiling build-up, hidden curtain tracks. Answers invented on site are the most common cause of rework and overspending.

  • you move the kitchen, bathroom or laundry — plumbing and ventilation drawings are needed
  • you plan lighting scenes, hidden lighting, underfloor heating in several zones
  • you order a kitchen, wardrobes or dressing rooms to custom sizes
  • you use stone, veneer, brass or complex ceilings that must be detailed
  • you want to fix the budget — an estimate without specifications is approximate
  • the crew is new to you, and drawings are your insurance

There is a financial argument too. An estimate is built on quantities: metres of cable, number of plumbing outlets, square metres of tiles and their layout, running metres of hidden lighting. Without working drawings these are guessed, and the difference shows up during the renovation as extra payments. In such cases the basic package with visualisations, working drawings and specifications makes more sense, and for complex projects the full package with elevations, furniture drawings and designer supervision. The document set is explained in What is included in an interior design project.

How do the design packages differ?

  • Planning: survey, 2–3 layout options, furniture layout, room schedule
  • Basic: everything in Planning + 3D visualisations of all rooms, working drawings for walls, electrics, plumbing, ceilings, floors and tile layout, specifications of materials and furniture
  • Full with designer supervision: everything in Basic + elevations of all walls, bespoke furniture drawings, site visits during the renovation on a schedule in the contract, selection of furniture, lighting and decor
  • purchasing and delivery of furniture is a separate furnishing service in any package

A simple test: if you can list every decision the crew will make on its own and you are fine with all of them, planning is enough. If the list is long or worries you, you need drawings.

How much do planning and a full project cost?

CAS Development rates: planning from $6/m², basic from $30/m², full with designer supervision from $45/m². The minimum budget for any package is $2,500, so for an ordinary flat the planning and basic packages may cost the same at the minimum — then it makes sense to take the package with drawings straight away.

What mistakes do people make when choosing a package?

  • taking only a plan and then asking the crew to "make it nice" without drawings
  • ordering a full project for a rental where a plan and simple finishes would do
  • approving a layout without furniture — nobody checked passages and wardrobe doors
  • skipping supervision where details are complex: stone, hidden doors, shadow gaps
  • comparing studios only by price per m² without checking the document set

If one team does the design and another does the renovation, designer supervision removes part of the risk: the designer visits the site and answers the crew's questions before something is done wrong.

Questions and answers

What is a space plan for an apartment?

A plan of walls, doors, rooms and furniture based on exact measurements. At CAS the planning package includes a survey, 2–3 layout options, a furniture layout and a room schedule.

Can I renovate with only a space plan?

Yes, if the renovation is simple: no relocated wet areas, complex electrics or bespoke furniture. For a major renovation, working drawings and specifications are better.

How much does a space plan cost?

At CAS from $6/m²; the minimum budget for any design package is $2,500.

How does the basic package differ from the full one?

Basic includes 3D visualisations, working drawings and specifications. Full adds wall elevations, bespoke furniture drawings and designer supervision during the renovation.
