General contractor or separate crews: which is better for a commercial property

CAS Development · 31 October 2026

A general contractor in Kyiv takes on the whole property, while separate crews give you more control but also more hassle. We look at how the two approaches differ for an office, shop or restaurant and when each one fits.

How does a general contractor differ from separate crews?

With separate crews, the client assembles the team: one contractor does the electrics, another the finishing, a third the plumbing or ventilation. There are as many contracts as crews, and coordination falls on you or on a specialist you hire.

A general contractor works differently: you sign one contract, and the contractor is responsible for the whole property — from demolition to handover. Who exactly does each type of work is the contractor's decision, but only they are answerable to you for the result.

When do separate crews make sense?

A multi-crew scheme can work if the scope is small, there are few types of work and you have the time and experience to coordinate everything. For example, when you only need to renew floor coverings or repaint the walls. Then an extra management layer is not needed.

But the more complex the property, the more interfaces between works. It is at these interfaces that disputes most often arise: who was supposed to close the chase, whose mistake is the floor level, who redoes whose work.

When does a commercial property need a general contractor?

Commercial premises earn money, so not only the result matters but also the order of works: services must be ready before finishing, and equipment and furniture before opening. If the crews are not coordinated, one waits for another and the premises stand idle.

  • one contract and one responsible party instead of several contractors, each responsible only for their own part;
  • a single estimate for the whole property — you see the full picture, not a set of separate offers that are hard to compare;
  • a common schedule in which works go in the right sequence, not as crews become free;
  • acceptance is simpler: claims go to one party rather than being split between contractors;
  • for commercial renovation it is more convenient when the contractor runs the property together with the design and procurement.

How much does a renovation with a general contractor cost?

CAS Development's guide rates are: office renovation from $350/m² (works and rough materials), turnkey renovation from $550/m², construction from $600/m², and construction with a turnkey fit-out from $1,100/m². These are rates per square metre, not a final price: finishing materials, furniture and appliances are costed separately per estimate.

For example: a 200 m² office at a rate from $350/m² is from $70,000 for works and rough materials. The exact sum comes from the estimate after measuring and design, not from a calculator like this. More on the office renovation cost page.

It is important to compare not only the price per metre but also what it includes. A separate crew's offer may look cheaper because it has no services, waste removal or procurement. These items will appear later anyway, but outside the contract.

What should the contract, estimate and reports include?

Whichever scheme you choose, fix the same things on paper: scope of works, an itemised estimate, a schedule, the payment order and warranty terms. With a general contractor this is one set of documents; with separate crews there are several, and they have to be aligned with each other.

  1. Ask for an itemised estimate rather than one total sum.
  2. Clarify what is included in the price and what is paid separately: finishing materials, furniture, appliances.
  3. Find out who is responsible for the result at the interfaces between different types of work.
  4. Agree how you will receive reports on the property.
  5. Check the warranty terms and how to make a claim.

If the property has a design project, for example in office design, the contractor builds the estimate and schedule from the drawings. This reduces the risk that decisions will have to be made right on site.

How do you choose the scheme for your property?

If the property is simple, there are few types of work and you are ready to coordinate the contractors yourself, separate crews can be considered. If the premises have building services, finishing, furniture and a fixed opening date, general contracting is usually more convenient: you talk to one person and get one result.

The decision depends not on the name of the scheme but on who is ultimately responsible for the interfaces between works. Ask every contractor this question and see what they answer and what they are ready to put into the contract.
