A brief for your interior designer: what to prepare before the first meeting

CAS Development · 1 November 2026

A brief for an interior designer is a short description of how you live and what you expect from the new space. The more precise it is, the fewer layout revisions you will go through and the sooner the project reaches a cost estimate. Here is what to put in it, plus a checklist you can fill in over one evening.

Why does an interior designer need a brief?

A brief makes the layout fit your daily life the first time: a designer cannot guess that you cook every evening, keep bikes at home or work from home twice a week. Without this, the layout ends up "average" and then gets redone: sockets move, wardrobes are added, the bedroom and the study swap places. A brief removes those loops before the first sketch.

It is not a questionnaire for its own sake. It is a technical assignment in plain language: who will live in the space, what will happen there, what needs storing, which habits matter and what budget to stay within. The designer adds clarifying questions at the first meeting, and you get a document to return to when you need to check a sketch against your original wishes.

A good brief helps you too. While filling it in, families often discuss for the first time who needs a study, whether a separate dining room matters and how much space clothes really take. It is better to agree now than in the middle of the renovation. How to choose the specialist is covered in How to choose an interior designer.

What should the brief say about family and routines?

Start with people, not rooms: describe each resident and an ordinary day. Zoning grows out of this: where quiet is needed, where guests gather, where sports gear goes.

  • how many people will live there, their ages and whether the family may grow
  • whether anyone works from home and needs a separate workplace with a door
  • pets: where they sleep, where to wash paws, where to keep food and the carrier
  • how often you host guests and whether they stay overnight
  • who cooks, how much and how often — this sets the size of the kitchen
  • the morning routine: how many people get ready at once and whether one bathroom is enough
  • hobbies and sport: tools, bikes, skis, musical instruments

If you are buying for several years ahead, write how the family may change. A nursery that becomes a teenager's room and a study that may turn into a guest room are planned differently from rooms "for ever".

What should the brief say about each room?

Go through every room and describe it in plain words, as you would explain to a friend — no need to be professional. The designer will translate it into layouts and drawings.

Hallway and storage

How many coats and pairs of shoes must be at hand in season, where to keep the pushchair, scooters, vacuum cleaner and drying rack. Whether you need a walk-in wardrobe, a pantry or a laundry. Storage is the most common reason for revisions: people remember it when the walls are already up.

Kitchen and dining

Which appliances you plan: built-in or freestanding, dishwasher, two ovens, wine cooler, coffee machine. Whether you need an island, how many people sit at the table on weekdays and on holidays. Describe what annoys you most in your current kitchen — it is the most useful part of the brief.

Living room, bedrooms and bathrooms

For the living room: whether you watch TV and from what distance, a projector, a fireplace, a place for books or a collection. For bedrooms: bed size, a dressing room, a dressing table, blackout curtains. For a child's room: age, play area, study corner. For bathrooms: bath or shower or both, double basin, wall-hung WC, space for the washing machine, and who uses which bathroom.

How do you show the designer a style you like?

Collect 15–20 interiors you like and a few you definitely dislike — you do not need to know the name of a style.

  • add one sentence to each picture: what exactly you like — colour, light, material, furniture
  • note which things move from your current home: furniture, paintings, collections, appliances
  • write about colours you are uncomfortable with and any material allergies
  • if you like natural wood, stone or brass, say whether you are ready to care for them

If you have not settled on a style yet, that is normal — see Interior design: where to start.

Which technical wishes go into the brief?

  • air conditioning and ventilation: which rooms, whether air purification is needed
  • underfloor heating: bathrooms, kitchen or the whole flat
  • lighting scenes, night lighting, control from the phone
  • smart home, video intercom, alarm, Wi-Fi across the whole area
  • water filters, a boiler for outages, backup power
  • sound insulation from neighbours or between rooms

Mark the items you are unsure about with a question mark. The designer will explain what to build in now and what can be added later without chasing walls.

Should the brief cover budget and timing?

Yes: the budget is part of the brief, not a secret from the designer. Write an approximate sum for the project, renovation and furniture, or at least an overall limit. For reference, an apartment design project at CAS starts at $30/m², minimum $2,500. Turnkey renovation starts at $550/m²: works and rough materials, while finishing materials, furniture and appliances are estimated separately. For an 80 m² flat: 80 m² × $550 = $44,000. You can estimate your own area in the renovation calculator; the exact figure comes from the estimate after the project.

Also give dates: when you want to move in and whether that is tied to selling another home or the start of the school year. Renovation timelines are set in the contract and depend on the scope.

Brief template: checklist before the first meeting

  • address, area, floor and condition: bare concrete, white box, resale home
  • who will live there, ages, pets, plans for the next few years
  • daily routines: who works from home, who cooks, how often guests come
  • list of rooms and wishes for each
  • what to store: clothes, sports gear, seasonal items, cleaning equipment
  • appliances and sanitary ware already chosen or bought
  • 15–20 references you like and a few you dislike
  • technical wishes, approximate budget and desired move-in date
  • who makes decisions and who approves sketches

The last point matters: if two people decide, both should attend key meetings, otherwise an approved layout will be reopened.

Typical brief mistakes

  • describing a magazine picture instead of the family's real life
  • hiding the budget in the hope the designer will "make it cheap"
  • forgetting about storage and household appliances
  • agreeing the brief with one family member and showing sketches to another

What happens after the brief?

After the first meeting the designer completes the brief, takes measurements and proposes several layouts, followed by the concept, visualisations, working drawings and specification. If you do not want to choose furniture, lighting and decor yourself, turnkey furnishing takes it on. If a layout alone is enough, read Space planning: when it is enough.

Questions and answers

Do I have to write the brief down?

No, the designer will gather part of the answers at the meeting. But a written brief saves time: the family agrees priorities in advance and the designer gets a document to check sketches against.

What if we don't know our style?

Collect pictures you like and dislike and note what catches you in them. The designer will suggest a style based on your references and lifestyle.

Should I name a budget at the first meeting?

Yes, at least roughly. Without a budget materials and furniture cannot be chosen, and the project risks costing more than you are ready to spend.

How much does a design project cost if the brief is ready?

It depends on area and scope: at CAS a design project starts at $30/m², minimum $2,500. A ready brief does not change the rate, but it cuts revisions.
