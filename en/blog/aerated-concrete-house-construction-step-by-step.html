Building an aerated concrete house step by step: what to check at each stage

CAS Development · 1 November 2026

Aerated concrete construction is less forgiving than it looks: the walls go up quickly, but most defects are built into a few details — the first course, lintels, ring beam, and the roof and window junctions. We go through the sequence of works the way a site manager runs it and, at each stage, show what the client should check while the work is still visible.

What is aerated concrete and what are the building stages?

Aerated concrete (autoclaved aerated concrete, AAC) is a lightweight masonry material with a large number of tiny pores. It holds its shape well, has precise dimensions and can be laid on thin-bed adhesive rather than a thick mortar joint. At the same time, aerated concrete is brittle in bending, absorbs water and does not tolerate uneven settlement of its base. That is why building with aerated concrete is not just about laying blocks but a system: a rigid base, reinforcement, cast-in-place ring beams, a facade protected from moisture and a continuous thermal envelope.

If you are still choosing the wall material, start with the comparison in the article timber frame, aerated concrete or timber beams. Here we assume the decision has been made and walk through building the shell and thermal envelope step by step.

  1. design and ground investigation
  2. foundation — most often a cast-in-place slab
  3. damp-proof course and the first course of blocks
  4. walls, bed-joint reinforcement and lintels
  5. ring beam and floor slabs
  6. roof
  7. windows, insulation and facade — closing the thermal envelope
  8. building services, plaster and screeds

Stage 0. Who decides the block grade, foundation and reinforcement?

The density and strength grade of the blocks, wall thickness, foundation type, reinforcement layout and the sizes of lintels and ring beams are set by the structural part of the design, not by the crew on site. This requires soil data: bearing capacity, groundwater level, frost heave susceptibility. On one plot a slab on a sand bed is enough, on the next one piles or a deeper foundation are needed. Without a ground investigation all these decisions are guesswork.

In the house design check that there are not only facades and floor plans but also foundation drawings, block layout with openings, ring beam, lintel and roof junction details, and a thermal design for the external envelope.

  • a ground investigation report for the specific building footprint
  • architectural and structural working drawings with a materials specification
  • details: plinth, first course, lintels, ring beam, wall plate, window reveals
  • a layout of service entries so ducts can be cast in before concreting
  • an estimate broken down by stage so actual costs can be compared with the plan

Stage 1. Why choose a slab foundation for aerated concrete?

For an aerated concrete house a cast-in-place reinforced concrete slab is often chosen: it works as a single rigid plate and reduces the risk of uneven settlement, which cracks aerated concrete masonry. The slab can be conventional or insulated (an insulated raft, the so-called Swedish slab), with extruded polystyrene under it and around the perimeter, and underfloor heating circuits within it. Whether a slab suits your plot is decided by the structural engineer based on the ground data.

What usually goes under the slab

  • topsoil removed and the base levelled
  • a bed of sand or crushed stone compacted in layers
  • geotextile so the layers do not mix
  • a blinding layer or another base for the waterproofing — as per the design
  • waterproofing and, for an insulated slab, a layer of XPS
  • two layers of reinforcement mesh on spacers with the required concrete cover

What to check at the foundation stage

  • the setting-out of axes and levels — an error here carries into every wall
  • compaction of every layer of the bed, not only the top one
  • rebar diameters, spacing and laps to the drawing, cover spacers
  • sleeves for drainage, water supply and the incoming cable — before concreting
  • the concrete class on the delivery notes, continuous pouring and vibration
  • curing: covering, keeping moist, frost protection; loading the slab only after it reaches the strength specified in the design

Stage 2. Why is the first course of blocks the most important?

The first course is the most important in the whole wall. A damp-proof course is laid between the foundation and the aerated concrete so moisture from the concrete and ground does not wick into the porous walls. The first course is laid on cement-sand mortar: its thickness takes up unevenness in the slab and sets a perfect horizontal line with a laser level. The following courses go on thin-bed adhesive — typically with a joint of just a few millimetres, and that is precisely the advantage of aerated concrete over ordinary mortar masonry.

  • the level of the first course is checked around the whole perimeter, not just at the corners
  • each course is planed or rubbed flat before adhesive is applied
  • adhesive is applied with a notched trowel across the full width of the block
  • the vertical joints of adjacent courses do not line up — bonding as per the design
  • blocks without tongue and groove are also glued on their vertical faces
  • bed-joint reinforcement — rebar in cut chases: as a rule in the first course, under window openings and every few courses as per the design

Stage 3. Why do openings need lintels?

Above door and window openings aerated concrete cannot work on its own: its bending strength is low. That is why lintels are installed — from U-blocks that act as permanent formwork for rebar and concrete, prefabricated reinforced lintels, or, for small openings, steel elements to calculation. The bearing length of the lintel on the wall is set by the design; market practice is at least a quarter of a metre on each side.

What to check when the walls go up

  • plumb walls and corners, opening sizes — to the window specification with an installation gap
  • reinforcement and concreting of lintels, insulation of the concrete part on the outside to avoid a cold bridge
  • ties between internal partitions and load-bearing walls — flexible anchors or rebar, as in the design
  • protecting the top of the masonry from rain during breaks: wet aerated concrete takes a long time to dry
  • work in cold weather — only with winter adhesive and within the temperatures allowed by the manufacturer

Stage 4. What is a ring beam for?

A ring beam is a cast-in-place reinforced concrete band poured on all load-bearing walls at floor level and under the roof. It ties the walls together, spreads point loads from floor slabs and rafters, and stops the masonry from spreading. U-blocks or thin aerated concrete blocks along the outer edge, with insulation between them and the concrete, often serve as formwork — so the ring beam does not become a cold strip on the facade.

Floors can be cast in place, made of precast hollow-core slabs or timber joists. Precast slabs are laid on aerated concrete only via a ring beam or a distribution beam; a cast-in-place floor is often poured together with the ring beam. The floor type affects spans, sound insulation and how services will be routed, so it is chosen at the design stage.

  • the ring beam is continuous around the whole perimeter, with L-shaped rebar at the corners
  • studs or anchors for the wall plate are cast into the ring beam under the roof before concreting
  • insulation in the outer part of the ring beam formwork
  • bearing of floor slabs — no less than in the design, and only on the ring beam

Stage 5. How is the roof fixed to aerated concrete walls?

The wall plate — the timber that carries the rafters — is fixed to the ring beam with cast-in studs and separated from the concrete by a waterproofing layer. Rafters, battens and membranes work as one system, and mistakes here show up as condensation, stains on the attic ceiling and damp insulation. For a pitched roof with a warm attic the build-up is usually: roof covering, battens, counter-battens with a ventilation gap, a breathable wind and water membrane, insulation between the rafters, a vapour barrier with taped joints, and the internal finish.

We covered the composition of each layer, typical thicknesses and the most common mistakes separately in the article on wall, floor and roof build-ups.

  • anchoring the wall plate and fixing rafters with metal brackets or straps as per the design
  • preservative and fire treatment of timber where specified
  • membrane overlaps and taping, the correct side of the breathable membrane
  • ventilation of the roof space: air inlet at the eaves, outlet at the ridge
  • junctions with walls, chimneys and vent stacks, gutters and snow guards

Stage 6. What is the thermal envelope and how are windows fitted?

The thermal envelope is a continuous shell of insulation and an airtight layer wrapping the house from the slab to the ridge. The weak points are not the wall surface but the details: the slab edge, ring beam, lintels, window reveals and the wall-to-roof junction. If the insulation is interrupted there, that is where cold spots, condensation and mould appear, even if the wall as a whole is thick.

The insulation thickness is set by the thermal calculation. With aerated concrete it matters that vapour permeability increases from the inner surface outwards: then moisture from the rooms passes through the wall instead of accumulating in it. That is why market practice on aerated concrete favours mineral wool insulation under thin-coat render or a ventilated facade; other solutions only to a calculation in the design.

  • windows are installed in three layers: an inner vapour-tight tape, installation foam, an outer breathable tape
  • where possible the window is moved into the insulation plane on brackets — fewer cold bridges
  • insulation fitted tightly with no gaps, staggered board joints and fixings to the pattern
  • the plinth and the lower part of the facade are protected from splashback and snow
  • walls must be dry before the facade goes on: covered aerated concrete releases water poorly

Stage 7. When do services, plaster and screeds start?

Once the roof and windows are in, the shell is weathertight and the services can go in: electrics, water supply and drainage, heating, ventilation. Chases in aerated concrete are cut with a wall chaser, without percussion tools, and special anchors for cellular concrete are used for wall-mounted equipment. After first-fix services come plaster and screeds — wet trades that add a lot of water to the house.

  • priming aerated concrete before plastering, since it quickly draws water out of the mix
  • reinforcing mesh at junctions of different materials — aerated concrete and the concrete ring beam
  • edge strip around the screed perimeter, especially with underfloor heating
  • ventilation and gradual drying before the finishes

What mistakes are made when building with aerated concrete?

  • saving on the ground investigation and using a “standard” foundation without calculation
  • laying the first course on adhesive without a levelling mortar — and unevenness carries through the whole wall
  • laying on ordinary mortar with a thick joint: the joint becomes a cold bridge
  • lintels of plain aerated concrete without reinforcement or with too short a bearing
  • a ring beam with breaks or without external insulation
  • aerated concrete left unprotected over winter, and a facade closed over a wet wall
  • unchecked window and roof junctions — the most common source of leaks and condensation

Which concealed works should be recorded with photos and sign-offs?

Everything that is covered by the next layer is worth accepting before it disappears from view: reinforcement of the slab and ring beams, waterproofing, the first course, lintels, membranes and vapour barrier, embedded items for services. Photos with a tape measure, a level and reference to the axes are the best record for the future. How to arrange an independent inspection is explained in the article on technical supervision; for a house the principle is the same.

  • a sign-off for the slab reinforcement before concreting
  • photos of the damp-proof course and first course with a level
  • reinforcement of lintels and ring beam, wall plate studs
  • membranes and vapour barrier with taped joints before boarding
  • window installation: perimeter tapes before the reveals are plastered
  • service sleeves and entries, pipework pressure tests

Questions and answers

What is the best foundation for an aerated concrete house?

There is no universal answer. A cast-in-place slab is often chosen because it is rigid and reduces the risk of uneven settlement, which cracks aerated concrete. The final decision — slab, strip or piles — is made by the structural engineer based on the ground investigation.

Is a ring beam mandatory in an aerated concrete house?

Yes, in a typical aerated concrete house a ring beam is cast at each floor level and under the roof: it ties the walls together, spreads loads from floor slabs and rafters and supports the wall plate. Its size and reinforcement are set by the design.

Does an aerated concrete house need insulation?

Most often yes: the insulation thickness is set by the thermal calculation. With aerated concrete, vapour permeability should increase outwards, so market practice favours mineral wool or a ventilated facade.

Can aerated concrete be laid in winter?

With limitations: using winter adhesive, within the manufacturer's temperatures, with the masonry protected from snow and moisture. Concrete work in winter needs separate curing measures. Often it is more economical to protect the shell over winter and continue in spring.

What is most important for the client to check during construction?

Concealed works: slab and ring beam reinforcement, the damp-proof course, the level of the first course, lintels, roof membranes and vapour barrier, window installation. They are best accepted with photos and sign-offs before being covered by the next layer.
